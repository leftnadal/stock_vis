## Purpose

The purpose of the Stock_vis Research Lab is to develop a better understanding of reality.

The Scientific Philosophy defines the highest-level attitudes and principles for researchers working toward this purpose. It guides researchers to give reality precedence over every model, treat models as tools for understanding reality, and continually advance understanding through research and evidence.

Models are core tools for developing a better understanding of reality, but models themselves are not the ultimate aim of the Research Lab.

## Scope

The Scientific Philosophy addresses the highest-level attitudes and principles that researchers in the Stock_vis Research Lab should follow in developing a better understanding of reality. It also defines the relationships among reality, understanding, models, research, evidence, and falsifiability within the Research Lab’s philosophical system.

These principles apply to both human researchers and non-human research systems. Throughout this document, “researcher” is used as a role term that includes both human researchers and non-human research systems engaged in research.

The Scientific Philosophy does not define specific research procedures, rules for research judgment, evaluation criteria, technical implementations, individual research topics, model-specific designs, or investment decision rules. These matters are addressed in the Research Methodology, Evaluation Methodology, Model Documents, and other lower-level documents.

The Scientific Philosophy provides the direction and standards with which lower-level documents must remain consistent, but it does not replace their specific content.

## How to Read the Principles

The five principles in this document are explained through a common structure to support consistent interpretation. This structure separates the statement of each principle from the reason it is needed, its precise meaning, its implications for research, and the misunderstandings most likely to arise around it.

These elements are not stages of a research process. They form an interpretive structure for clarifying what each principle requires and for preventing philosophical principles from being confused with concrete methodology.

### Principle

States one of the Research Lab’s highest-level principles in a concise and memorable form.

A Principle does not include concrete procedures or exception conditions.

### Why

Explains why the principle is necessary.

It identifies the ways research may drift from reality, misuse models or evidence, or depart from its purpose when the principle is absent.

### Meaning

Defines the precise meaning and scope of the principle.

It explains the core concepts, the attitude required by the principle, its boundaries of application, and any necessary relationships to the other principles. It does not define concrete research procedures or execution rules.

### Research Implications

Explains how accepting the principle should shape the researcher’s stance and judgment, as well as the direction of research.

Research Implications identify the direction that the philosophy gives to research. Concrete steps, judgment rules, and evaluation criteria are defined in the Research Methodology and Evaluation Methodology.

### Common Misunderstandings

Identifies recurring misinterpretations of the principle and corrects them.

This section does not introduce new principles or exceptions beyond those established in Meaning. Its role is to clarify the boundaries of the approved meaning.

## The Philosophical System

The five principles of the Scientific Philosophy do not form a collection of independent declarations. Together, they constitute a coherent system through which understanding of reality can develop.

The system is expressed through five principles:

1. Reality takes precedence over every model.
2. Models exist as tools for understanding reality.
3. Research begins with unexplained reality.
4. Better evidence advances understanding.
5. Every model must be falsifiable.

Within this system, **reality is the object of continuing observation and the final standard against which every model and current understanding must be judged**. Developing a better understanding of reality is the central purpose of the Research Lab. Models are core tools for understanding reality and expressing current understanding.

Researchers observe reality through their current models and understanding. However, gaps may emerge between reality and what the current understanding can adequately explain. These aspects of reality constitute **unexplained reality**. They reveal the limits of current understanding, and research begins from these explanatory gaps.

Through research, evidence is sought, examined, and interpreted. Better evidence advances understanding through critical examination and interpretation. As understanding advances, an existing model may be retained or strengthened, but it may also be revised, restricted in scope, replaced, or discarded. The resulting understanding and models then provide a renewed basis for observing reality.

**Falsifiability is not the final stage of this cycle.** It is a condition that keeps every model open to meaningful challenge from reality and evidence, thereby preventing understanding from becoming fixed around an existing explanation.

The working relationship can be summarized as follows:

```text
Observe reality through current models and understanding
→ Identify an explanatory gap between reality and current understanding
→ Begin research from unexplained reality
→ Seek, critically examine, and interpret evidence
→ Advance understanding
→ Retain, revise, restrict, replace, or discard models
→ Observe reality again through the advanced understanding and models
```

This structure does not define the stages or execution order of the Research Protocol. It is a philosophical conceptual structure that explains how the five principles relate to one another around the central purpose of developing a better understanding of reality.

## Principle 1: Reality

> **Reality takes precedence over every model.**

### Why

Models are essential tools for understanding reality, but they can also constrain how researchers perceive it.

When a model has been used for a long time or has performed well in the past, researchers may begin to treat aspects of reality that the model cannot explain as insignificant exceptions or noise. They may interpret observations in ways that preserve the existing model or exclude conflicting evidence in order to protect current understanding.

When this happens, research ceases to be an effort to understand reality and becomes an effort to defend an existing model.

The researcher’s ultimate allegiance must therefore be to the reality that the model is intended to understand, not to the model itself.

### Meaning

Researchers observe reality through their current models and understanding. A model, however, does not define reality, and reality does not exist to conform to a model.

When an aspect of reality cannot be adequately explained by the current model, researchers must not dismiss the discrepancy or exclude it from investigation merely to preserve that model. Unexplained reality may reveal a limit in the current model or understanding and may indicate the need for further research.

Such a discrepancy may arise from many possible sources, including:

- error in observation or measurement;
- limitations or bias in the available data;
- an incorrect assumption within the model;
- a limited scope of applicability;
- a change in reality;
- a relationship or phenomenon not yet represented in the current model; or
- a structure of reality that requires a fundamentally different understanding.

Which explanation is correct cannot be known before investigation.

Giving reality precedence over models does not mean assuming in advance that the model is wrong or that every observation is correct. It means refusing to exclude a discrepancy from investigation merely to preserve the current model.

The researcher’s allegiance is not to the model that has been built, but to the reality that the model is meant to understand.

### Research Implications

An explanatory gap between a model and reality should be treated not merely as noise or error to be dismissed, but as a potential research signal. It must not be excluded from investigation merely to preserve the current model.

Not every discrepancy must become a formal research project. The criteria and procedures for determining which observations become Research Triggers are defined in the Research Methodology.

Investigation may ultimately support or strengthen the existing model. It may instead lead to the model being revised, restricted in scope, replaced, or discarded.

The principle does not determine the conclusion in advance. It requires that discrepancies between reality and a model remain open to investigation.

### Common Misunderstandings

#### “An observed value or event always represents reality accurately.”

It does not. Observations, measurements, and data provide evidence about reality, but they may contain error, delay, bias, or incompleteness. A particular observation may represent only one aspect of reality and is not identical to reality as a whole.

#### “A model must be rejected as soon as one conflicting observation appears.”

It need not be. A discrepancy is a signal for investigation, not an automatic instruction to reject the model. The reliability of the observation, the assumptions and scope of the model, and the possibility of change in reality must all be examined.

#### “Giving reality precedence means that models should not be trusted.”

It does not. Models are core tools for understanding reality. This principle does not reject models; it prevents them from being given authority over the reality they are intended to understand.

#### “Every unexplained phenomenon must become a research project.”

It need not. Unexplained reality may provide a potential starting point for research, but its importance, impact, recurrence, feasibility of investigation, and relevance to the purpose of the Research Lab must be considered. The concrete criteria for this decision belong to the Research Methodology.

## Principle 2: Model

> **Models exist as tools for understanding reality.**

### Why

Models are core tools for understanding reality. However, as the work of building and improving models continues, a model can easily become an end rather than a means.

Researchers may begin to focus on a model’s performance, complexity, sophistication, or technical novelty without asking whether understanding of reality has actually advanced. Improvements in measurable metrics may be treated as equivalent to improvements in understanding, and the continued advancement of the model itself may come to be regarded as a research achievement.

When this happens, the center of research shifts from reality to the model. Rather than using the model to understand reality, researchers risk treating reality as a means of demonstrating the model’s performance or legitimacy.

The Scientific Philosophy must therefore make the role of models explicit: models are means to better understanding, not ends in themselves.

### Meaning

A model is not reality itself. It selects, simplifies, and structures aspects of reality so that they can be understood.

Every model therefore involves choices about what to include, what to omit, which relationships to represent, and which assumptions to make. Because a model represents reality from a particular perspective and for a particular purpose, these choices shape the understanding that the model can support.

Selection and simplification are not, by themselves, defects in a model. The purpose of a model is not to reproduce the whole of reality without reduction. Researchers must nevertheless remain aware that every model represents only part of reality and operates within particular assumptions, conditions, and limits.

A model’s performance, accuracy, complexity, explanatory power, or efficiency may all be important. None of these qualities, however, is the ultimate aim of the Research Lab. Their research significance lies in how they contribute to a better understanding of reality.

A model’s output is also not identical to understanding. A model may reveal patterns, relationships, possibilities, or differences. Its outputs contribute to better understanding only when they are connected to evidence and the context of reality, critically interpreted, and considered together with the model’s limitations.

The first principle defines what takes precedence when a model and reality diverge. The second principle defines why models exist.

- Reality is the final standard.
- Understanding is the central purpose of the Research Lab.
- Models are core tools for pursuing that purpose.

Models do not replace or define reality, and they must not become ends in themselves.

### Research Implications

Accepting this principle requires the development, selection, and use of models to remain connected to understanding of reality.

Researchers should not ask only:

> Has this model’s performance improved?

They must also ask:

> Does this model actually advance understanding of reality?

Improved performance may contribute to better understanding, but the two do not always coincide. A model may perform better according to a particular metric while failing to reveal important structures in reality, obscuring its range of applicability, or concealing the conditions under which it may fail.

Conversely, a simple or limited model may have substantial research value if it reveals an important relationship, exposes a limitation in current understanding, or enables better questions and further research.

Models should therefore not be treated as objects that must be preserved or advanced for their own sake. In response to better evidence and advanced understanding, an existing model may be retained or strengthened, but it may also be revised, restricted in scope, replaced, or discarded.

The Evaluation Methodology and Model Documents define the specific criteria by which a model’s contribution is evaluated, the forms in which its assumptions and scope are recorded, and the methods by which different models are compared.

### Common Misunderstandings

#### “The model with the highest performance is always the best model.”

Not necessarily. Performance may be important, but no single metric can fully determine whether a model advances understanding of reality. Its applicability, limitations, stability, and relationship to reality must also be considered.

#### “A more complex and sophisticated model necessarily provides a better understanding of reality.”

It does not. Complexity may be necessary to represent important structures in reality, but complexity itself does not guarantee better understanding. Unnecessary complexity may make it more difficult to determine what the model captures and what it misses.

#### “A model should reproduce reality as completely as possible.”

It need not. A model selects and simplifies reality for a purpose. What matters is not whether it reproduces the whole of reality, but what understanding its selections make possible and what limitations they introduce.

#### “A model whose internal operation cannot be fully explained should not be used.”

Not necessarily. A model that is difficult to interpret internally may still help identify important patterns or possibilities in reality. Its output, however, does not automatically constitute understanding. It must be examined together with evidence, the context of reality, and the model’s limitations.

#### “If models exist for understanding, prediction is unimportant.”

It is not. Prediction can be an important way to test whether a model captures aspects of reality and to connect current understanding with future observation. Predictive performance, however, must not become the ultimate aim of the Research Lab.

## Principle 3: Research

> **Research begins with unexplained reality.**

### Why

Research Questions can be generated without limit. Not every question, however, investigates an important problem identified in reality.

Researchers may begin with available data, a preferred method, or a model they wish to improve, and then construct a question around it. They may pursue measurable performance gains without first identifying what remains unexplained or unresolved in reality. Such work may produce technically sophisticated results without meaningfully advancing understanding of reality.

When a question is treated as the starting point, researchers may focus on how to answer it before establishing why it matters. Research can then become an effort to justify a predetermined question, dataset, or method rather than an investigation of a genuine gap in current understanding.

The origin of research must therefore lie not in the question a researcher wishes to ask, but in an aspect of reality that current understanding cannot adequately explain or resolve.

### Meaning

Unexplained reality does not refer only to a completely novel phenomenon for which no explanation has ever been proposed.

An explanation may already exist but remain insufficient for the present reality. Existing models or evidence may conflict. A change in the external environment may make prior understanding uncertain or incomplete. A new possibility may emerge whose meaning, conditions, or implications are not yet understood. A knowledge gap or unmet need may reveal something that current understanding or existing methods cannot adequately address.

Examples may include:

- a mismatch between what a model leads researchers to expect and what is actually observed;
- an existing explanation that fails to account for an important aspect of reality;
- conflicting models or bodies of evidence;
- a change in reality that places the applicability of existing understanding in doubt;
- a new possibility whose meaning or conditions are not yet understood; or
- an important gap in knowledge or an unresolved need.

Here, **explain** does not refer only to causal explanation. It includes adequately understanding relevant states, relationships, changes, significance, and unresolved conditions in reality.

The principle can therefore be interpreted as follows:

> **Research begins when reality reveals a limit in current understanding.**

A research undertaking does not originate in a Research Question alone. An aspect of reality is first recognized as a Research Trigger. The unresolved condition revealed by that trigger is framed as a Research Problem, and the specific object of investigation is then formulated as a Research Question.

```text
Reality
→ Recognize a Research Trigger
→ Frame a Research Problem
→ Formulate a Research Question
```

The definitions, classifications, and subsequent operational stages of this process are established in the Research Methodology. The Scientific Philosophy establishes the prior relationship: **unresolved reality comes before the question constructed to investigate it.**

In this principle, “begins” refers to the origin and justification of a research undertaking, not to the formal Research stage defined in the Research Protocol.

The first principle determines what takes precedence when reality and a model diverge. The third principle determines where the explanatory gap revealed by that divergence should lead.

- Reality takes precedence over the current model.
- Unexplained reality may provide the origin of research.
- Research investigates the gap so that understanding can advance.

### Research Implications

Every research undertaking must be traceable to an unresolved condition identified in reality.

Before formulating a Research Question or Hypothesis, researchers should be able to explain the following relationship:

```text
What has been observed in reality?
→ What can current understanding not adequately explain or resolve?
→ What Research Problem does this reveal?
→ What specifically must be investigated?
```

A Research Question does not stand alone. Its research justification comes from the Research Problem it investigates and from the aspect of reality that gave rise to that problem.

Data, methods, and models may all make research possible. Their availability, however, does not by itself justify a research undertaking. A method should be selected because it helps investigate a problem in reality; a problem should not be constructed merely to provide an occasion to use a preferred method.

A potential Research Trigger does not automatically become a formal research project. Its significance, relevance to the purpose of the Research Lab, feasibility of investigation, expected impact, and priority must still be considered. The specific criteria and procedures for making that determination belong to the Research Methodology.

### Common Misunderstandings

#### “Research begins with a good question.”

This is incomplete. A Research Question is essential, but it is not the original source of the research undertaking. In the Stock_vis Research Lab, a Research Question is formulated to investigate a Research Problem that arose from a Research Trigger in reality.

#### “Researchers should not formulate their own questions.”

That is not what the principle means. Researchers must formulate questions in order to make a problem investigable. The question, however, must remain connected to the unresolved condition in reality from which the research originated.

#### “Unexplained reality means only a failure of an existing model.”

It does not. Unexplained reality may become visible through an unexpected observation or failure, but also through external change, a new possibility, a gap in knowledge, or an unmet need. In each case, research begins by identifying what current understanding cannot adequately explain or resolve.

#### “Every unexplained phenomenon must become a research project.”

It need not. Unexplained reality provides a potential origin for research. Whether it becomes a formal research project depends on its importance, impact, relevance, feasibility of investigation, and priority.

#### “A problem that is already known cannot be researched again.”

It can. An existing explanation may no longer fit current conditions, new evidence may conflict with it, or an established solution may prove insufficient. A known problem can therefore reveal a new limit in current understanding and become the origin of further research.

## Principle 4: Evidence

> **Better evidence advances understanding.**

### Why

Researchers may use evidence to advance understanding of reality, but they may also use it to justify conclusions and models they already prefer.

When researchers become strongly committed to current understanding or a particular model, they may give greater weight to supporting evidence while treating conflicting evidence as an exception or error. They may select only evidence that fits an existing conclusion or strengthen confidence through the accumulation of evidence without adequately examining its quality or independence.

When this happens, evidence no longer serves as a basis for changing understanding. It becomes an instrument for protecting what is already believed, and research becomes an effort to defend a predetermined conclusion rather than to understand reality.

Research organizations may also mistake an increase in available data for an increase in understanding. More evidence does not necessarily produce better understanding. Evidence that is inaccurate, biased, duplicated, or derived from the same underlying source may merely reinforce misplaced confidence.

The Research Lab must therefore value evidence not by its quantity or its agreement with an existing conclusion, but by its capacity to support, challenge, and advance understanding of reality.

### Meaning

Evidence is not reality itself. Evidence consists of observations, measurements, data, records, documents, experimental results, comparisons, and other grounds that bear on claims and understanding about reality.

Evidence may contain error, delay, bias, incompleteness, or misleading interpretation. The mere existence of information does not make it reliable evidence.

Better evidence does not simply mean:

- more data;
- more recent information;
- information from a more prominent source;
- observations that agree more closely with the current model; or
- results produced by a more complex analysis.

Evidence is better when, in the context of the reality and claims under investigation, it provides stronger grounds for supporting or challenging current understanding, distinguishes more effectively among competing explanations, or reveals aspects of reality that previous evidence failed to capture.

What constitutes better evidence may vary across research contexts. Reliability, relevance, independence, correspondence with current reality, and the capacity to distinguish among alternative explanations may all matter. The specific criteria and procedures for evaluating these qualities are defined in the Evaluation Methodology.

Better evidence does not always strengthen current understanding. Depending on its relationship to existing models and explanations, it may lead to:

```text
strengthening current understanding
→ refining its details
→ revising particular assumptions or relationships
→ restricting its scope
→ restructuring the overall explanation
→ discarding the existing understanding
→ forming a fundamentally different understanding
```

Better understanding is therefore not necessarily an improved version of the previous understanding. Abandoning an existing explanation and adopting a different structure may provide a better account of reality.

Evidence also does not produce understanding automatically. It must be gathered, examined in relation to its source and context, compared with other evidence and alternative explanations, and critically interpreted.

The principle can therefore be understood as follows:

> **Better evidence advances understanding through critical examination and interpretation.**

The first principle establishes reality as the final standard. The second defines models as tools for understanding reality. The third establishes unexplained reality as the origin of research. The fourth requires evidence examined in research to be capable of substantively affecting current understanding.

### Research Implications

Evidence must not be gathered merely to support a conclusion that has already been chosen. It must be treated as capable of supporting, revising, restricting, restructuring, or overturning current understanding.

Researchers must therefore consider not only evidence that supports a preferred model or hypothesis, but also evidence that conflicts with it or supports an alternative explanation.

```text
Evidence is consistent with current understanding
→ It may strengthen that understanding

Evidence conflicts with current understanding
→ Both the evidence and the current understanding must be re-examined

Better evidence weakens current understanding
→ Revision, restriction, restructuring, replacement, or abandonment must remain possible
```

Conflicting evidence does not automatically require the immediate rejection of current understanding. Researchers must examine the reliability and relevance of the new evidence, its relationship to existing evidence, the scope of the model, and the plausibility of alternative explanations.

If evidence never affects a conclusion, researchers should examine whether it is actually being allowed to update understanding.

The research value of evidence does not arise merely because it has been collected, cited, or retained. Its significance lies in how it affects current understanding: what it strengthens, what it weakens, what it changes, and what it reveals for the first time.

The following matters are defined in lower-level documents:

- how sources and provenance are recorded;
- how reliability, relevance, and independence are evaluated;
- how conflicting bodies of evidence are compared;
- how uncertainty and confidence are represented; and
- how the effects of new evidence on models and understanding are documented.

The Scientific Philosophy does not prescribe these procedures. It requires that evidence remain capable of substantively changing understanding.

### Common Misunderstandings

#### “More or newer evidence is always better evidence.”

Not necessarily. Additional evidence may be duplicated, dependent on the same underlying source, biased in the same direction, or less relevant to the question being investigated. Recent information is not automatically more reliable or more informative. Quality and research relevance cannot be replaced by volume or recency.

#### “Better evidence only makes the existing model more accurate or sophisticated.”

It may do so, but it need not. Better evidence may strengthen or refine an existing model, but it may also restrict its scope, reveal that its assumptions are inadequate, require a different model, or lead to a fundamentally different understanding.

#### “Only quantitative data can count as evidence.”

That is not the case. Quantitative measurements may provide important evidence, but documents, observations, experimental findings, comparisons, and other verifiable records may also serve as evidence. What qualifies as evidence depends on the research context and the claim under examination.

#### “Evidence speaks for itself.”

It does not. Evidence must be examined in relation to its source, context, assumptions, limitations, and alternative explanations. The same evidence may be compatible with more than one interpretation, making critical comparison and judgment necessary.

#### “Evidence that agrees with current understanding is better evidence.”

It is not necessarily better. Agreement with an existing model does not establish quality. Evidence that conflicts with current understanding may be more important if it reveals a part of reality that the current model has failed to capture.

## Principle 5: Falsifiability

> **Every model must be falsifiable.**

### Why

Even if reality is given precedence over every model, and even if better evidence is expected to advance understanding, these principles cannot function when a model can accommodate every possible outcome.

A model may claim success when an expected result occurs, but explain an opposing result as a temporary exception, a delayed effect, or a condition that was not previously stated. When no expected change occurs, the model may claim that reality has not yet reflected its prediction. Conflicting evidence may be dismissed as noise, and additional conditions may be introduced after every failure.

A model constructed in this way cannot be meaningfully challenged by reality. Whatever happens, the model can be preserved.

When a model is protected from every possible challenge, new evidence cannot substantively affect it. Research then becomes an effort to defend an existing explanation rather than an effort to advance understanding of reality.

Every model must therefore leave open the possibility that reality and evidence may show it to be wrong, incomplete, or valid only within a narrower scope than previously believed.

### Meaning

Falsifiability does not mean that a model must be false. Nor does it mean that a model must be discarded as soon as a single conflicting observation appears.

A model is falsifiable when its claims, assumptions, scope, and expected relationships are stated clearly enough that some observations or evidence could, in principle, count meaningfully against it.

The central requirement is:

> **A model must remain open to the possibility of being shown wrong or inadequate by reality.**

A conflict between a model and reality may have several possible explanations. The observation or measurement may be unreliable. The available data may be incomplete. The model may have been applied outside its intended scope. One of its assumptions may be wrong, or the model itself may no longer provide an adequate account of reality.

The outcome of investigation is therefore not predetermined.

```text
Conflict with reality or evidence
→ Examine the observation, measurement, and data
→ Examine the model’s assumptions and scope
→ Determine whether the conflict reveals a limitation or failure
→ Retain, revise, restrict in scope, replace, or discard the model
```

Falsifiability requires that these outcomes remain genuinely possible. It does not require that every conflict lead to rejection.

The form and rigor with which falsifiability is specified and tested may differ according to a model’s type and maturity. An early exploratory model may not yet have a complete validation design, but it must still make claims that are contestable in principle. As the model develops, its tests, scope, limitations, and failure conditions must become more explicit.

Falsifiability is also not limited to models that make numerical or future predictions. Models that explain relationships, describe states and changes, or support decisions must also make it possible to identify evidence that would weaken their claims or reveal their limitations.

The fifth principle enables the preceding four principles to function:

- Reality can take precedence only if models can be challenged by reality.
- Models can remain tools for understanding only if they can change as understanding advances.
- Research can begin from unexplained reality only if discrepancies are not excluded merely to protect an existing model.
- Better evidence can advance understanding only if it is capable of weakening or overturning existing models.

Falsifiability is therefore not the final stage of research. It is a condition that keeps models open to challenge from reality and evidence, thereby preventing understanding from becoming fixed around an existing explanation.

### Research Implications

Accepting this principle requires researchers to consider not only how a model may succeed, but also how it may fail.

As a model develops, researchers should make the following increasingly explicit:

- what the model claims about reality;
- the assumptions on which those claims depend;
- the conditions and scope within which the model is expected to apply;
- the observations or evidence that support the model;
- the observations or evidence that would meaningfully challenge it; and
- what must be reconsidered when reality diverges from the model.

This does not require the Scientific Philosophy to prescribe a particular validation method, document template, statistical test, or threshold. The Research Methodology, Evaluation Methodology, and Model Documents define the concrete forms in which claims, scope, limitations, and failure conditions are recorded and evaluated.

The philosophical requirement is more fundamental:

> **Both the possibility of a model’s success and the possibility of its failure must be expressible.**

A model may be legitimately revised after conflicting evidence is discovered. New evidence may reveal a previously unknown condition, boundary, or relationship that justifies changing the model.

The problem is not revision itself. The problem arises when every failure is absorbed through an unstated exception or an after-the-fact change that prevents the model from ever being judged inadequate.

Material revisions must be explicit about the evidence that prompted them and about the claims, assumptions, or scope that changed. The required recording format is defined in the Research Methodology and Model Documents.

A model that has not yet been falsified must still be treated as provisional. Surviving previous challenges may increase confidence in a model, but it does not establish permanent truth or guarantee that the model will remain valid under future conditions.

### Common Misunderstandings

#### “A model is falsified whenever one conflicting observation appears.”

Not necessarily. A conflicting observation may arise from measurement error, incomplete data, random variation, or application outside the model’s intended scope. The observation and the model must both be critically examined before a conclusion is reached.

#### “A model that has not been falsified is true.”

It is not necessarily true. It has survived the challenges applied so far. This may justify greater confidence, but it does not establish permanent validity. Further observations of reality and new evidence may still reveal limitations or failures.

#### “Exploratory models do not need to be falsifiable.”

They are not exempt. An early exploratory model may not yet have fully specified claims or precise failure conditions, but it must be capable of developing toward a form that can be meaningfully challenged. Exploratory status cannot become a permanent shield from evaluation.

#### “Models of complex reality cannot be falsifiable.”

Complexity may make evaluation more difficult, and a single observation may be insufficient to judge a model. Complexity, however, does not justify allowing a model to remain compatible with every possible outcome. The more complex the model, the more clearly its claims, scope, uncertainty, and limitations must be expressed.

#### “Falsifiability applies only to predictive models.”

It does not. Models that explain relationships, represent states and changes, or support decisions may not always make a single numerical prediction. They must nevertheless make it possible to identify evidence that would weaken their claims, restrict their scope, or reveal that another model provides a better account of reality.

#### “Adding new conditions after a model fails is always improper.”

Not necessarily. New evidence may legitimately reveal a condition or boundary that was previously unknown. Revising the model may therefore advance understanding.

What is improper is repeatedly adding exceptions solely to prevent the model from ever being challenged. A legitimate revision must be explicit, evidence-based, and open to further testing.

## Relationship to Research Methodology

The Founding Principles define Stock_vis’s ultimate purpose and highest-level commitments. The Scientific Philosophy operates within that foundation. It defines the attitudes and principles that researchers must follow as the Research Lab develops a better understanding of reality in service of the broader purpose established by the Founding Principles.

The Scientific Philosophy defines what research should value and the standards toward which it must remain oriented. It establishes the Research Lab’s highest-level relationship to reality, models, research, evidence, and falsifiability. It does not prescribe concrete research procedures or operational judgment rules.

The Research Methodology translates these principles into repeatable research processes and judgment rules. It defines how the principles are enacted in practice, including how Research Triggers are recognized, framed as Research Problems, and formulated as Research Questions; how evidence is sought and examined; and how models and understanding are revised.

```text
Scientific Philosophy
→ What research should value and remain oriented toward

Research Methodology
→ How those principles are enacted in research practice
```

The Research Methodology must not establish an independent purpose or silently redefine the Scientific Philosophy. When a methodological need conflicts with an existing philosophical principle, the conflict must be made explicit, its effects analyzed, and the relevant documents revised only through an approved change.

The Evaluation Methodology defines the principles, criteria, and validation procedures used to judge whether research processes, evidence, models, and decision-support outputs remain consistent with the Scientific Philosophy. At the operational level, it addresses matters such as evidence quality, research validity, model performance, scope, limitations, and falsifiability.

```text
Scientific Philosophy
→ Provides the highest-level direction for evaluation

Evaluation Methodology
→ Defines how research and models are judged in accordance with that direction
```

Model Documents record how individual models apply and are tested against the Scientific Philosophy. They document each model’s purpose, assumptions, evidence and data, implementation, scope, limitations, evaluation results, and version history. They should also show how the model contributes to understanding reality and how it remains open to meaningful challenge.

The overall document hierarchy is:

```text
Founding Principles
→ Scientific Philosophy
→ Research Methodology
→ Evaluation Methodology
→ Model Documents
```

This hierarchy does not mean that higher-level documents predetermine every lower-level choice.

Research, evaluation, and model development may reveal limitations in the current Methodology or Scientific Philosophy. When they do, the limitation or conflict must be made explicit, its consequences analyzed, and the relevant higher-level document reconsidered through discussion and approved revision.

Lower-level documents must not silently ignore or redefine higher-level principles.

The Scientific Philosophy applies to both human researchers and non-human research systems. It does not directly prescribe the concrete behavior of a particular research system.

Concrete behavior rules are defined in the Research Methodology, which translates the Research Implications of this document into procedures and judgment rules. The Evaluation Methodology then examines whether those behaviors and their results remain consistent with the Scientific Philosophy.

```text
Scientific Philosophy
→ Principles shared by human and non-human research systems

Research Methodology
→ Translation of those principles into concrete behavior and judgment

Evaluation Methodology
```
